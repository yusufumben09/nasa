    Data and method

    The route engine uses real terrain cells derived from CTX and HiRISE. The model does not generate slope or elevation.

    The AI does not change the route; it only turns the verified metrics into an English route summary and navigation advice.

    What affects how far a vehicle travels?

    The factors that set how far a vehicle travels on sloped ground, and how each one changes on Mars.

    01 · Initial speed (v₀)

    This is the strongest factor. Distance grows with the square of speed: double the speed and the distance roughly quadruples.

    On Mars: Rovers are very slow; Perseverance tops out at about 150 metres per hour. At that speed the vehicle stops almost instantly.
    02 · Slope angle (θ)

    The steeper the ramp, the larger the component of gravity along the path (mg·sinθ).

    • A vehicle going uphill slows down sooner and travels less.
    • A vehicle going downhill accelerates faster.
    On Mars: Curiosity and Perseverance were designed to withstand a 45° tilt without tipping over, but drivers avoid slopes steeper than 30°. This simulation's default limit is even more cautious: 20°.
    03 · Friction coefficient (μ)

    It depends on the surface (asphalt, ice, soil) and the state of the tyres. The friction force is μ·mg·cosθ, so more friction means a shorter distance.

    On Mars: The surface is loose sand, dust and rock. Wheels slip in soft sand, which is why the yellow areas on the map are penalised.
    04 · Gravitational acceleration (g)

    On Earth it is about 9.8 m/s². With weaker gravity, a vehicle travels further on the same ramp.

    On Mars: g ≈ 3.72 m/s². At the same speed on the same ramp a vehicle travels about 2.6 times as far as on Earth; on the Moon (1.62 m/s²) the ratio is about 6. Braking distance grows by the same ratio.
    05 · Air resistance

    Its effect grows with speed, the vehicle's shape (aerodynamics) and its frontal area.

    On Mars: The atmosphere is about 1.6% as dense as Earth's; at the same speed air resistance is about 60 times smaller and is negligible at rover speeds.
    06 · Rolling resistance

    It depends on tyre pressure, tyre type and the vehicle's weight.

    On Mars: Rovers have no pneumatic tyres; Curiosity and Perseverance use aluminium wheels. Rolling resistance rises quickly as a wheel sinks into soft ground.
    07 · Motor power and brakes

    If the vehicle is driven by a motor or is braking, these also directly set the distance.

    On Mars: Rovers are driven by electric motors. The radioisotope generator on Curiosity and Perseverance produces about 110 W; power is limited, so the energy cost of every climb matters.
    A note on mass

    In the ideal case, with no air resistance, mass does not affect the distance. Gravity and friction are both proportional to mass, so mass cancels out of the equation.

    On Mars: Air resistance is nearly zero, so this ideal case holds better on Mars than on Earth.

    Lessons from real missions

    Every penalty in the simulation comes from a problem that really happened on Mars.

    Cross slope

    Going uphill is not the same as going across a slope

    On the same slope, a rover driving straight up slips and uses more energy, but its tip-over risk is low. Driving sideways across the slope tilts the body; the risk of tipping and sliding sideways grows.

    That is why the algorithm splits slope into an along-track and a cross-track component and penalises each separately.

    along = g·u   cross = |g × u|
    Soft sand

    Flat but dangerous

    Sand fields are almost flat, so they look harmless on a slope map. But wheels can sink in.

    In 2005 Opportunity got stuck in a sand ripple named “Purgatory” and took about five weeks to free. In 2009 Spirit got stuck in soft soil at “Troy”; it could not be freed and contact was lost in 2010.

    Rocks

    Wheel damage

    Sharp rocks puncture wheels; large rocks catch under the body. When holes and tears appeared faster than expected on Curiosity's aluminium wheels in 2013, the team began routing it over softer ground.

    Rock density is highest on crater rims (impact ejecta) and on steep ridge slopes.

    Mapping

    Elevation models from orbit

    The HiRISE camera on Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter images the surface at about 25–30 cm per pixel. Images taken from two angles produce a digital elevation model; slope and hazard maps are derived from it.

    Autonomy

    A rover that decides on the ground

    The team on Earth plans the long route with orbital maps. Perseverance then uses its AutoNav system to see the terrain ahead with its own cameras and avoids nearby obstacles by itself while driving.
